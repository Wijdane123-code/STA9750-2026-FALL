load_netflix_data <- function(){
  library(tidyverse)
  library(rvest)
  library(readxl)
  library(fs)
  
  data_dir <- path("data", "mp01")
    
  if(!dir_exists(data_dir)){
    dir_create(data_dir, showWarnings=FALSE, recurse=TRUE)
  }
  
  NETFLIX_URLS <- list(
    `2023H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-a-netflix-engagement-report",
    `2023H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2023",
    `2024H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2024",
    `2024H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2024",
    `2025H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2025",
    `2025H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2025",
    `2026H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2026"
  )
  
  download_if_needed <- function(url, local){
    if(!file_exists(local)){
      download.file(url, local, quiet = TRUE, mode = "wb")
    }
    
    return(local)
  }
    
  NETFLIX_URLS |> 
    map(read_html) |> 
    map(html_element, xpath = "//a[contains(@href, '.xlsx')]") |> 
    map_chr(html_attr, "href") |> 
    enframe("period", "url") |> 
   mutate(path = path(data_dir, paste0(period, ".xlsx")), 
          path = map2(url, path, download_if_needed), 
          data = map(path, read_excel, skip=3, na="*"), 
          data = map(data, \(x) x |> mutate(`Release Date` = as.Date(`Release Date`)))) |>
    unnest(data) |> 
    select(-url, -path) |> 
    mutate(year=str_sub(period, 1, 4), 
           half=str_sub(period, start=6), 
           period_start = make_date(year, if_else(half==1, 1, 7), 1), 
           period_end = make_date(year, if_else(half==1, 6, 12), if_else(half==1, 30, 31))) |> 
    filter(!is.na(`Hours Viewed`)) |> 
    mutate(live_event = !is.na(Runtime) & str_detect(Runtime, "\\d+:\\d+\\*"), 
           clean_time = str_remove(Runtime, fixed("*")),
           Runtime = 60 * hour(hm(clean_time)) + minute(hm(clean_time))) |>
    select(-clean_time) |>
    mutate(Runtime = coalesce(Runtime, mean(Runtime, na.rm=TRUE)), 
           .by=Title, 
           Title = stringi::stri_trans_general(Title, "Latin-ASCII"),
           Title = str_remove(Title, " // .*"), 
           Title = str_remove(Title, "[^[:ascii:]].*"), 
           Title = str_trim(Title)) |>
    filter_out(Title == "") |>
    select(year, 
           period, 
           period_start, 
           period_end, 
           Title, 
           -half, 
           Runtime, 
           Views, 
           `Hours Viewed`, 
           `Release Date`,
           `Available Globally?`, 
           live_event) |>
    rename(title = Title,
           runtime = Runtime,
           views = Views,
           hours_viewed = `Hours Viewed`,
           release_date = `Release Date`,
           available_globally = `Available Globally?`) |>
    mutate(year = as.integer(year),
           available_globally = available_globally == "Yes")
}

The initial exploration shows that the Netflix dataset contains 61,892 rows and 11 columns. The data includes information about titles, reporting periods, run time, views, hours viewed, release dates, global availability, and live events. The dataset covers seven reporting periods, from January 2023 to June 2026. One important finding is that several columns have missing values. The release_date column has 35,251 missing values, meaning that only about 43% of the records contain a release date. The runtime column has 13,085 missing values, while views has 18,198 missing values. These gaps may limit the analysis of release dates, runtime, and viewing activity, so the results should be interpreted carefully. The data also shows that most titles are not available globally. Approximately 35% of records are marked as globally available, compared with about 65% that are not. In addition, only 310 records are identified as live events, making them a very small portion of the dataset. Finally, the hours_viewed variable shows a large difference between the mean and median. The average is approximately 8.3 million hours, while the median is 1.8 million hours. The maximum is approximately 964 million hours. This suggests that viewing hours are unevenly distributed, with a small number of highly popular titles receiving much more viewing time than most other titles. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of checking missing values and understanding the distribution of viewing hours before conducting further analysis.

Strategy Memo

To: Netflix Leadership

From: Wijdane Kouri

Date: October 15, 2026

Subject: Recommendations for Netflix’s Original Content Strategy for 2027 and Beyond

In 2026H1, TV shows accounted for 94.7% of viewing, compared with 5.3% for movies. Looking at the earlier period movies accounted for 4.2% of viewing. This comparison helps Netflix understand movie viewing over time and decide how to balance its original content investments. TV shows should remain a major focus because series can keep viewers interested across multiple episodes and seasons, giving them a reason to continue watching Netflix. However this does not mean Netflix should stop investing in movies. Movies can attract large audiences, create excitement around new releases, and reach viewers who prefer a complete story they can watch in one sitting. For 2027 and beyond, Netflix should prioritize high quality TV series while continuing to invest in movies that show strong audience potential. Instead of treating every production as equally valuable, the company should use viewing data to identify which types of content attract the most attention and deliver value for the money spent. Netflix should also consider production budgets when deciding which projects to approve. A series that generates substantial viewing over several episodes may justify a larger investment, while a movie that attracts a strong audience at a reasonable cost can also be valuable. At the same time, viewing numbers should not be the only measure of success. Netflix should consider whether a title attracts new subscribers, encourages existing members to stay, and appeals to audiences in different countries. The company should also examine how long people continue watching a title after its release rather than judging performance only during the first few days. Overall, Netflix should use a balanced approach that protects its investment in successful TV series while leaving room for distinctive and well targeted movies. By comparing viewing performance, production costs, and subscriber engagement, Netflix can make more informed decisions about its original content budget and build a content library that appeals to different audiences.

Live events are a relatively new part of Netflix’s viewing data and their share of total viewing has increased over time, although it remains small. In 2023H1, 2023H2, and 2024H1, live events accounted for 0% of total viewing. They first appeared in 2024H2, when they represented 0.35% of viewing. This share increased to 0.46% in 2026H1. However, the increase was not consistent throughout every period. Live events reached approximately 0.42% in 2025H1 before dropping to 0.31% in 2025H2 and rising again to 0.46% in 2026H1. This shows that live events have gained some ground, but they still represent less than 1% of total viewing hours. Therefore, Netflix should be careful not to treat this growth as evidence that live events will become a major part of its overall viewing.

The comparison between live events and pre-taped content also shows why Netflix should look beyond total viewing share. The median viewing for live events was 1.7 million hours, compared with 1.8 million hours for pre-taped content. These figures are close, suggesting that the typical title in each category can attract a similar amount of viewing. However the difference becomes much larger when looking at the 90th percentile, which represents titles performing better than approximately 90% of titles in their category. Live events reached 6.5 million hours at this level, while pre-taped content reached 20.0 million hours. In other words, the higher performing pre-taped titles attracted more than three times as many viewing hours as the comparable live events.

For Netflix’s 2027 strategy, these findings suggest that live events deserve continued attention, but they should not automatically receive the same priority as pre-taped content. The similar median figures show that live events can generate meaningful viewing, while the much lower 90th percentile suggests that they have not matched the stronger viewing results achieved by pre-taped titles. Netflix should continue comparing the performance of both categories across future periods and use both median and higher-end viewing figures to guide its decisions. This would allow leadership to recognize the potential of live events without overlooking the stronger viewing performance shown by the top tier of pre-taped content.
